## Unit 1: Emergence of Authoritarian States

### 1.1: IB Papers and Key Concepts

* **IB exam day**: Monday, May 3, 2027

* **IB papers**
    * **Paper 1 (20%)**: source-based; 1 hour; 24 marks; Prescribed Subject 3: The Move to Global War
        * Case studies: Japan (1931–1941) and Germany/Italy (1933–1940); know both
        * **Q1a (3 marks)**: comprehend/extract from one source (usually text); three distinct points; no own knowledge
        * **Q1b (2 marks)**: interpret message of a source (usually visual); two distinct points; no own knowledge
        * **Q2 (4 marks)**: OPCVL
        * **Q3 (6 marks)**: compare and contrast
        * **Q4 (9 marks)**: sources + own knowledge
    * **Paper 2 (25%)**: essay; 1.5 hours; 30 marks; 15-point markband
        * Topic 10: Authoritarian States + Topic 12: Cold War
        * Must compare leaders from two different IB regions
            * Hitler (Europe), Castro (the Americas), Mao (Asia and Oceania)
        * Cold War pairing: Mao + Nixon; Berlin Blockade + Cuban Missile Crisis
    * **Paper 3 (35%)** and **IA (20%)**: completed in 11th grade

* **Six IB key concepts**
    * **Change**: what shifted; gradual vs. sudden
    * **Continuity**: what stayed the same, and why
    * **Causation**: short-term vs. long-term causes
    * **Consequence**: intended vs. unintended results
    * **Significance**: why the event matters historically
    * **Perspectives**: whose viewpoint; who is left out

* **Authoritarian state**
    * Power concentrated in a single leader/small group
    * Citizens' rights restricted; opposition suppressed legally, by force, or both
    * **Totalitarianism**: extreme end; state controls political, economic, social, and cultural life

### 1.2: Historiography and Command Terms

* **Historiography**: study of how history is written and interpreted
    * **Orthodox**: traditional, established interpretation
    * **Revisionist**: challenges orthodox view with new evidence
    * **Post-revisionist**: synthesizes both; more nuanced
    * Integrate historian debates into arguments, not appended at the end

* **Command terms**
    * **Evaluate**: weigh strengths and limitations; reach a judgment
    * **To what extent**: how far something is true; argue a position
    * **Compare and contrast**: similarities and differences throughout
    * **Examine**: dig into assumptions/relationships; more than describe
    * **Discuss**: balanced review of arguments; conclusions with evidence

### 1.3: Conditions for the Emergence of Authoritarian States

* **IB's four conditions**: economic factors, social division, impact of war, weakness of political system
    * Create opportunity; do not guarantee authoritarianism
    * Conditions + leader + methods = authoritarian state
    * Class teaches five: economic crisis, political instability, nationalism/wounded pride, post-WWI trauma, weak democratic institutions

* **Economic factors**: economic crisis → loss of faith in democracy → opening for extremists
    * **Germany**: hyperinflation (1923); 6 million unemployed by 1932 (1 in 3 workers); Great Depression (1929–1933)
    * **Cuba**: by 1958, 75% of arable land owned by foreign corporations
    * **China**: Western powers controlled key ports/trade

* **Impact of war**
    * WWI: 20 million dead; empires collapsed
    * Defeated nations: humiliation, debt, territorial loss (Article 231)
    * Victors: disillusionment (Italy's "mutilated victory")
    * Veterans available for paramilitaries (Freikorps)
    * **Cuba**: Batista's U.S.-backed dictatorship created a culture of violence
    * **China**: humiliation by 1895 defeat to Japan, Boxer Uprising suppression, Shandong betrayal at Versailles

* **Social division**
    * Class conflict; fear of communism pushed elites toward authoritarian "stability"
    * **Germany**: middle class ruined by hyperinflation
    * **Cuba**: landowners/foreign corporations vs. landless peasants
    * **China**: landlord-peasant divide; 80% rural and poor

* **Weak political systems**
    * **Germany**: proportional representation → unstable coalitions; 20+ governments (1919–1933); Article 48
    * **Cuba**: Batista's 1952 coup cancelled elections and suspended constitution
    * **China**: Republic of China (1912) collapsed into warlordism

* **Methods to establish authoritarian states**
    * **Persuasion**: speeches, rallies, radio, promises
    * **Propaganda**: posters, newspapers, film, youth movements
    * **Coercion**: intimidation, paramilitary violence
    * **Use of force**: armed takeovers, purges
    * **Legal methods**: using existing laws to dismantle democracy; most effective long-term
    * Most durable states combined all methods

### 1.4: Weimar Germany

* **End of WWI**
    * Germany surrendered November 11, 1918; Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated → power vacuum
    * **Weimar Republic**: Germany's first democracy; named after city where constitution was written

* **Stab-in-the-back myth (Dolchstoßlegende)**: Germany "undefeated," betrayed by socialists and Jews at home
    * False; military leaders knew war was lost but civilians took the blame
    * Used relentlessly by Hitler

* **Treaty of Versailles (1919)**
    * **Article 231 (War Guilt Clause)**: sole responsibility; legal basis for reparations
    * **Reparations**: 132 billion gold marks (£6.6 billion)
    * **Territorial losses**: Alsace-Lorraine to France; West Prussia/Posen to Poland (Polish Corridor cut off East Prussia); Memel to Lithuania; all colonies
    * **Military**: army 100,000; no air force; no tanks; navy restricted
    * **Rhineland**: demilitarized; Allied occupation for 15 years
    * **[Sally Marks, revisionist]**: Versailles was lenient compared to Brest-Litovsk (1918), which took ⅓ of Russia's European territory and 90% of its coal mines

* **Early crises (1919–1923)**
    * **1919 Spartacist Uprising**: communist revolt; crushed by Freikorps
    * **1920 Kapp Putsch**: right-wing coup; army refused to defend Republic; defeated by general strike
    * **1921**: reparations bill announced
    * **1923 Ruhr occupation**: France occupies Ruhr after German default
    * **1923 hyperinflation**: $1 = 4.2 trillion marks; savings wiped out; ruined middle class later loyal to Hitler
    * **November 1923 Beer Hall Putsch**: Munich; modeled on Mussolini's March on Rome; sentenced to 5 years, served 9 months; wrote *Mein Kampf*

* **"Golden Years" (1924–1929)**
    * **Gustav Stresemann**: Chancellor, then Foreign Minister
    * **Rentenmark**: new currency; ended hyperinflation
    * **Dawes Plan (1924)**: U.S. loans; restructured reparations; dangerous dependency on U.S. money
    * **Locarno Treaty (1925)**: accepted western borders
    * **League of Nations (1926)**: Germany joins
    * **Young Plan (1929)**: reparations reduced, spread over 59 years
    * Nazi seats collapsed: 32 (1924) → 12 (1928)
    * Prosperity = democratic stability; crisis = extremist opportunity

### 1.5: Hitler's Rise to Power

* **Munich (1919)**
    * Bavarian Soviet Republic (April–May 1919) suppressed by Freikorps
    * Magnet for nationalists/veterans/anti-communists
    * Army used Hitler as informant → discovered the DAP

* **DAP → NSDAP**
    * **German Workers' Party (DAP)**: founded January 5, 1919 by Anton Drexler
    * Hitler joins September 1919; member #555 (actually ~55th; numbers started at 501)
    * **NSDAP**: founded February 24, 1920
    * **Twenty-Five Point Programme**: nationalism, anti-Semitism, anti-Marxism, rejection of Versailles/parliamentary democracy; a recruitment tool
    * Hitler party chairman July 29, 1921 after threatening to resign
    * **[Kershaw]**: appeal was articulating audience's resentments back to them; "projection screen for mass anxiety"

* **Nazi ideology**
    * **Pan-Germanism**: unite all ethnic Germans (Austria, Sudetenland, Eastern Europe) into a Greater German Reich
    * **Lebensraum ("living space")**: expand east into Slavic land (Poland, Ukraine, Russia)
    * **Racial hierarchy**: "Aryans" at top; Jews as "race tuberculosis"
    * **Anti-Semitism**: Jews as scapegoat for defeat, economic crises, communism
    * **Anti-communism**: communism as Jewish conspiracy; USSR as main enemy
    * **Führerprinzip ("leader principle")**: absolute authority in one man
    * **[Kershaw]**: ideology consistent from *Mein Kampf* (1925) to end of WWII

* **Great Depression**
    * October 1929 Wall Street Crash → U.S. recalled Dawes loans
    * Unemployment 1.3 million (1929) → 6 million (1932); 30%+ of workforce
    * Chancellor Brüning ruled by emergency decree (**Article 48**: President bypasses Reichstag); normalized irrelevance of parliament

* **Electoral breakthrough**
    * **1930**: 107 seats (18.3%), up from 12
    * **July 1932**: 230 seats (37.4%); largest party, no majority
    * **November 1932**: dropped to 196 seats (33.1%); losing momentum/money
    * **1932 presidential election**: lost to Hindenburg; 13 million votes (36%) in runoff
    * Built on: reversing Versailles, scapegoating, rallies, radio, SA intimidation

* **Backroom deals**
    * **Franz von Papen**: dismissed as Chancellor December 1932, replaced by **Kurt von Schleicher**; sought revenge
    * Von Papen proposed Hitler as Chancellor, himself as Vice-Chancellor
        * "Within two months we will have pushed Hitler so far into a corner he will squeak."
        * "We've hired him."
    * **Hindenburg**: 85; called Hitler "the Bohemian corporal"; had refused him twice (August and November 1932)
    * **January 30, 1933**: Hitler appointed Chancellor; only 3 Nazis of 12 ministers
    * Hitler refused Vice-Chancellorship; wanted legal appearance for legitimacy
    * **[Richard Evans]**: not inevitable; required complicity of conservative elites who underestimated him
    * **[Bullock]**: combined ideological conviction with tactical opportunism

* **Was Hitler's rise inevitable?**
    * **Structuralist [Ian Kershaw]**: conditions created a window Hitler stepped through; without them he's a footnote (*Hitler: 1889–1936 Hubris*, 1998)
    * **Intentionalist [Lucy Dawidowicz]**: *Mein Kampf* was a programme pursued consistently; Holocaust/war always the destination (*The War Against the Jews*, 1975)

### 1.6: Castro's Rise to Power

* **Cuba before Castro**
    * Independence from Spain 1898
    * **Platt Amendment (1901)**: U.S. right to intervene written into Cuban constitution; repealed 1934
    * U.S. corporations owned ~70% of sugar industry; utilities, railroads
    * **Sugar monoculture**: ~200,000 workers unemployed 6+ months/year ("dead season")
    * 30%+ adult illiteracy; Havana casinos vs. rural poverty
    * Formally independent but a U.S. client state
    * **[Richard Gott]**: "a culture of endemic corruption and political violence"

* **Batista**
    * **Fulgencio Batista**: dominated politics from 1933; president 1940–1944
    * **March 1952 coup**: cancelled elections he'd likely lose; U.S. recognized in 17 days
    * Corruption; secret police (**SIM**); torture
    * Closed the legal path to power

* **Fidel Castro**: born August 13, 1926; son of plantation owner; law degree (University of Havana); running for Congress when coup cancelled elections

* **Moncada, exile, and the Granma**
    * **July 26, 1953 Moncada Barracks attack**: ~160 fighters; failed; ~60+ executed
    * Sentenced to 15 years; 4-hour defense speech: "History will absolve me"
    * **26th of July Movement (M-26-7)**: named after attack
    * 1955: released; exile in Mexico; met Che Guevara
    * **December 2, 1956 Granma landing**: 82 fighters; ~20 survived; retreated to Sierra Maestra
    * Military failure, propaganda triumph (compare Beer Hall Putsch trial)
    * **[Hugh Thomas]**: victory was Batista's self-destruction as much as rebel success

* **Guerrilla war (1957–1959)**
    * Strategy: make regime ungovernable
    * Peasant support through land reform promises, schools, clinics
    * Urban M-26-7: bombings, assassinations, strikes
    * Rival strategies failed: Directorio Revolucionario palace attack (March 1957); Frank País killed (July 1957); 1958 general strike
    * **Radio Rebelde (1958)**: broadcast across Cuba
    * **March 1958**: U.S. arms embargo on Batista
    * **December 1958**: Guevara captures Santa Clara; Cuba cut in two
    * **January 1, 1959**: Batista fled; Castro entered Havana January 8
    * **[Gott]**: revolution was a moral collapse of the old regime
    * **[Georgie Anne Geyer]**: Castro power-hungry from the start; revolutionary rhetoric a vehicle for his will



<br>



### 1.7: Mao's Rise to Power

* **China's conditions**: unequal treaties (Opium Wars, 1842+); warlord era after 1916; Japanese aggression from 1931; driven by imperial humiliation

* **Chinese Communist Party (CCP)**: founded July 23, 1921, Shanghai; Mao a founding member
    * CCP-GMD cooperation 1923–1927
    * **Shanghai Massacre (April 1927)**: GMD massacres CCP → CCP to countryside

* **Mao's innovation**: revolution built on peasantry, not urban proletariat
    * "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun" (1938)
    * **[Jonathan Spence]**: adapted Marxism-Leninism to Chinese conditions

* **Long March (October 1934–October 1935)**: ~86,000 began; ~8,000 reached Yan'an; founding epic
    * **Zunyi Conference (January 1935)**: Mao consolidates leadership

* **Victory**: Civil War resumes 1945–1949; Chiang Kai-shek retreats to Taiwan
    * **October 1, 1949**: Mao proclaims the PRC from Tiananmen Gate
    * **[Spence]**: victory political, not primarily military; GMD lost will to govern



<br>



### 1.8: Comparing Conditions

* **Economic conditions**
    * **Germany**: hyperinflation + Depression; ruined middle class
    * **Cuba**: sugar monoculture; U.S. domination; seasonal unemployment
    * **China**: semi-colonial extraction; warlord fragmentation; GMD hyperinflation; peasant poverty
    * Necessary but not sufficient; create opportunity

* **Role of war**
    * **Hitler**: WWI defeat + Versailles resentment
    * **Castro**: revolutionary violence; U.S. Cold War support of Batista
    * **Mao**: warlord wars, civil war, Sino-Japanese War; each grew CCP base

* **Political weakness**
    * **Germany**: proportional representation; Article 48; elites undermined democracy
    * **Cuba**: 1952 coup destroyed electoral legitimacy
    * **China**: warlordism; corrupt GMD

* **Legal vs. armed path**: Batista's coup pushed Castro to armed insurrection; Beer Hall Putsch failure pushed Hitler to legal path

## Unit 2: Consolidation and Nature of Authoritarian States

### 2.1: Hitler's Consolidation

* **Four methods**: legal measures, use of force, charismatic leadership, propaganda; all used simultaneously
    * Total dictatorship by August 2, 1934; 18 months after appointment
    * Required collaboration of elites, army, judiciary, civil service

* **Reichstag Fire (February 27, 1933)**
    * Dutch communist Marinus van der Lubbe arrested; evidence suggests he acted alone
    * **Reichstag Fire Decree (February 28)**: suspended free speech, press, assembly, privacy; indefinite detention without trial

* **Enabling Act (March 23, 1933)**
    * Needed ⅔ majority; SA/SS surrounded Kroll Opera House; communists already arrested
    * Passed 444–94 (only SPD against)
    * Hitler could make laws without the Reichstag for 4 years
    * Democracy voted itself out of existence
    * **[Evans]**: constitutional foundation of dictatorship; under duress but technically legal

* **Gleichschaltung ("bringing into line")**: coordinating all institutions under Nazi control
    * **May 1933**: trade unions dissolved → German Labour Front (DAF)
    * **July 1933**: Law against the Formation of New Parties; one-party state
    * **January 1934**: Law for Reconstruction of the Reich; state parliaments abolished

* **Night of the Long Knives (June 30–July 2, 1934)**
    * **Ernst Röhm** (SA leader) wanted "second revolution" and SA to replace army
    * Hitler chose army; SS killed ~200+ (Röhm, SA leaders, von Schleicher)
    * Courts accepted "treasonous plot" justification
    * **[Kershaw]**: rule of law was now whatever Hitler said

* **Führereid (August 2, 1934)**
    * Hindenburg died; Hitler merged Chancellor and President → **Führer und Reichskanzler**
    * Armed forces swore personal oath of unconditional obedience to Hitler by name
    * Made later military resistance legally/psychologically catastrophic



<br>



### 2.2: The Nazi State

* **SS (Schutzstaffel)**: under Heinrich Himmler; began as bodyguard; "state within a state"
    * By 1939 controlled Gestapo, concentration camps, SD (Security Service), Waffen-SS
    * Required proof of "Aryan" ancestry to 1750 (1800 for others); checked by Reich Office for Kinship Research
    * **Dachau**: opened March 22, 1933; first inmates political prisoners
    * Institutional backbone of the racial state

* **Gestapo (Geheime Staatspolizei)**: secret police; created April 1933; nationalized under Himmler 1936
    * ~32,000 employees for 80 million Germans (1939)
    * Relied on voluntary denunciations
    * **[Robert Gellately]**: "self-policing society" (*Backing Hitler*, 2001)

* **Propaganda**
    * **Joseph Goebbels**: Minister of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda (March 1933)
    * **Volksempfänger ("People's Receiver")**: cheap radio; 70%+ of households by 1939
    * **Triumph of the Will (1935)**: Leni Riefenstahl; Nuremberg Rally film
    * **Nuremberg Rallies**: annual spectacles of unity
    * Banned "degenerate" art, jazz, modernist literature

* **Cult of the Führer**: political religion; Hitler as Providence-sent savior
    * "If only the Führer knew": blamed subordinates, not Hitler
    * **[Kershaw]**: Führer myth was the central mobilizing force; popular legitimacy outran terror

* **Economic and social policy**
    * Unemployment 6 million (1932) → ~300,000 (1938) via rearmament
    * **Autobahn**; public works
    * **Hjalmar Schacht**: Economics Minister 1934–37; secret **Mefo bills** for rearmament
    * **Hitler Youth / League of German Girls (BDM)**; **Strength Through Joy (KdF)**: cheap vacations
    * **Nuremberg Laws (1935)**: Jews stripped of citizenship; Jewish-Aryan marriage banned
    * Economy heading toward insolvency; needed conquest

* **"Working towards the Führer" [Kershaw]**
    * Hitler avoided paperwork; few written orders
    * **Polycratic**: competing power centers (SS, SA, Wehrmacht, party, civil service)
    * Officials anticipated Hitler's wishes; most radical proposal won favor → self-radicalizing system



<br>



### 2.3: Castro's Consolidation

* **Three phases** (slower and more ideologically ambiguous than Hitler)
    * **1959**: broad nationalist coalition; not yet socialist
    * **1960–61**: nationalization + Bay of Pigs; U.S. pressure radicalizes
    * **December 1961**: declared Marxist-Leninist
    * Key question: ideology or U.S. hostility?
        * **[Gott]** (*Cuba: A New History*, 2004): nationalist/anti-imperial; Marxism a geopolitical response
        * **[Geyer]** (*Guerrilla Prince*, 1991): ambitious and power-driven from the start

* **Revolutionary tribunals (1959)**
    * ~550 executions of Batista-era officials (mostly SIM); many televised
    * **[Thomas]** (*Cuba: A History*, 1971): broadly popular; genuine legitimacy

* **Agrarian Reform Law (May 17, 1959)**
    * Broke up estates over 400 hectares; to peasants/state cooperatives
    * ~$1 billion U.S. assets nationalized
    * Eisenhower → CIA planning Castro's removal; Cuba turns to USSR (1960 trade deals; Soviet oil)
    * Made U.S.-Cuba conflict inevitable
    * **[Thomas]**: as much reactive as ideological

* **Nationalization and U.S. break (1960)**
    * All U.S. businesses nationalized (oil refineries refused to refine Soviet crude); ~$1 billion
    * U.S. cut sugar quota; cut diplomatic relations (January 1961); trained Brigade 2506
    * **Committees for the Defense of the Revolution (CDRs)**: September 28, 1960; neighborhood surveillance; Cuba's version of voluntary denunciation

* **1961 Literacy Campaign**
    * ~100,000 student volunteers to rural Cuba; ~700,000 adults learned to read
    * Literacy ~60–70% → 96%+ in one year
    * Genuine achievement and consolidation tool (gratitude, loyalty, dependency)



<br>



### 2.4: The Nature of Castro's Cuba

* **Bay of Pigs (April 17, 1961)**
    * CIA-trained Brigade 2506 (~1,400); Kennedy withdrew air cover
    * Defeated within 72 hours; 1,189 captured; ransomed for ~$53 million in food/medicine
    * Castro declared Cuba socialist on eve of invasion
    * **[Gott]**: greatest political gift Castro received; justified every security measure

* **Marxist-Leninist declaration (December 2, 1961)**: "I am a Marxist-Leninist and will be one until the last day of my life."
    * Communist Party of Cuba (**PCC**) the only legal party
    * **[Gott]**: genuine ideological evolution from U.S. pressure
    * **[Geyer]**: Marxism a convenient framework for Soviet support and one-man rule

* **Political control**
    * One-party state
    * **CDRs**: one per city block
    * All media nationalized
    * **G2 (State Security)**: Cuba's Gestapo
    * **UMAP camps (1965–68)**: forced labor for homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses, "socially deviant"
    * **[Thomas]**: "authoritarian but not totalitarian"

* **Consolidation bargain**: political freedom traded for social gains
    * **Healthcare**: universal; 3,000+ of 6,000 doctors fled by 1962; infant mortality fell
    * **Education**: free university; school enrollment doubled
    * **Racial equality**: discrimination abolished; Gini coefficient improved
    * **Weaknesses**: embargo shortages; sugar dependency; Soviet subsidies (~$4–6 billion/year by 1970s)

* **Women**
    * **Federation of Cuban Women (FMC)**: August 23, 1960
    * Workforce participation ~13% → ~38%
    * Equal pay; abortion legal (1965)
    * Machismo and domestic labor persisted



<br>



### 2.5: Mao's Consolidation

* **PRC proclaimed (October 1, 1949)**: "The Chinese people have stood up."
    * Inherited 12 years of war (Sino-Japanese War 1937–45; Civil War 1945–49)
    * Tasks: unify China (Tibet, Xinjiang), rebuild economy, consolidate CCP control
    * One-party state from day one; PLA integrated into party
    * **[Spence]** (*The Search for Modern China*, 1990): revolution + national reconstruction

* **Land reform (1950–1952)**
    * **Agrarian Reform Law (June 1950)**: ~43% of farmland redistributed
    * **Struggle sessions**: peasants denounce landlords; outcomes predetermined
    * ~1–2 million landlords killed
    * **Marriage Law (1950)**: banned arranged marriage, concubinage, bride-price; women could divorce
    * **[Frank Dikötter]** (*The Tragedy of Liberation*, 2013): deliberate class warfare; mass complicity in violence

* **Campaign to Suppress Counter-Revolutionaries (1950–1953)**
    * Targeted GMD officials, landlords, religious leaders, spies; accelerated by Korean War
    * ~500,000–2 million executed; **laogai** labor camps
    * **Quota system**: execution rate of 1 per 1,000 in cities; distinct from Hitler's/Castro's targeted terror
    * Mass and diffuse by design → collective complicity

* **Three-Anti (1951) and Five-Anti (1952) Campaigns**
    * **Three-Anti**: corruption, waste, bureaucratism in the party
    * **Five-Anti**: urban bourgeoisie; bribery, tax evasion, theft of state property, cheating contracts, stealing state info
    * Ended private business; dismantled every opposition class

* **Korean War (1950–1953)**
    * October 1950: People's Volunteer Army enters; armistice July 1953
    * 180,000+ Chinese killed (including Mao's son, Mao Anying)
    * "Resist America, Aid Korea"; justified repression; nationalist legitimacy



<br>



### 2.6: Mao's Policies and the Great Leap Forward

* **First Five-Year Plan (1953–1957)**
    * Soviet model; 156 projects; ~10,000 Soviet advisers
    * Heavy industry; steel roughly doubled
    * Collectivization 90%+ by 1956
    * Agriculture neglected
    * **Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship (1950)**

* **Hundred Flowers Campaign (1956–1957)**
    * Response to Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" (1956)
    * "Let a hundred flowers bloom; let a hundred schools of thought contend"
    * Criticism far exceeded expectations
    * Debate: deliberate trap vs. genuine miscalculation
    * **[Roderick MacFarquhar]** (*Origins of the Cultural Revolution*, 1974): wanted controlled criticism, not a trap

* **Anti-Rightist Campaign (1957–1958)**
    * ~500,000–550,000 labeled "rightists"; to laogai/re-education
    * Ended intellectual freedom for a generation
    * Officials learned never to tell Mao bad news → false reporting
    * **[Dikötter]**: destroyed feedback mechanisms; made Great Leap deaths inevitable

* **Great Leap Forward (1958–1962)**
    * Goal: industrial communist society within 15 years
    * **People's Communes**: 500 million peasants in ~26,000 communes; private plots abolished; communal kitchens
    * **Backyard furnaces**: melted tools and pots; steel unusable
    * Inflated statistics; grain exported to USSR while China starved
    * **Famine (1959–1962)**: 15–55 million deaths; largest in recorded history
    * **[Dikötter]** (*Mao's Great Famine*, 2010): ~45 million; Mao knew and continued
    * **[Maurice Meisner]** (*Mao's China and After*, 1999): genuine ideological project; failure of method, not intent

* **Comparing consolidation**

    | Feature | Hitler | Castro | Mao |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Scale of killing | ~200 (Night of Long Knives) + camps | ~550 executions (1959) | 1–2 million (land reform); 500k–2M (campaigns) |
    | Method of terror | Targeted enemies; SS/Gestapo | Batista loyalists; CDRs | Quota-based mass campaigns; struggle sessions |
    | External threat | Communist conspiracy; Jews | U.S.; Bay of Pigs | U.S.; Korean War |
    | Economy | Rearmament-driven recovery | Nationalization; Soviet dependency | Five-Year Plan; Great Leap catastrophe |
    | Women | Kinder, Küche, Kirche | FMC; workforce; abortion | Marriage Law; workforce |
    | Legitimacy | Economic success | Nationalist/anti-imperial | Military victory + peasant revolution |

    * Mao's the most violent and most structurally thorough



<br>



### 2.7: The Cultural Revolution

* **Causes (1966)**
    * Mao sidelined after Great Leap
    * **Liu Shaoqi** (President) and **Deng Xiaoping** (General Secretary): pragmatic recovery policies; "revisionism" to Mao
    * Fear of new "bourgeois" bureaucrat class
    * Goals: reassert personal control; purge rivals
    * Not about culture; mass mobilization to destroy rivals

* **Red Guards (1966–1969)**
    * Millions of students; schools closed
    * **Four Olds**: old culture, customs, habits, ideas
    * Temples, art, books destroyed; teachers/officials humiliated, beaten, killed
    * Factions fought each other
    * 1969: PLA restored order; Red Guards "sent down" to countryside
    * **[Spence]**: most destructive episode of CCP rule

* **Key events and victims**
    * **Liu Shaoqi**: died in prison 1969
    * **Deng Xiaoping**: purged twice; survived
    * **Sending down**: ~16–17 million urban youth to villages
    * **Little Red Book (*Quotations from Chairman Mao*)**: mandatory
    * **Lin Biao**: named successor 1969; died in plane crash fleeing to USSR (1971)
    * 500,000–2 million killed
    * Universities closed 1966–1976
    * **Gang of Four** (Jiang Qing + three): arrested weeks after Mao's death (September 9, 1976)

* **Cults of personality**
    * **Hitler**: quasi-religious Führer myth; spectacle; demanded emotional submission
    * **Castro**: charisma; 4–8 hour speeches; revolutionary mythology; "Socialism or Death"; demanded participatory endorsement
        * **[Gott]**: legitimacy earned through revolution more than spectacle
    * **Mao**: most extensive; demanded internal transformation
        * **[Dikötter]**: citizens had to publicly perform belief

### 2.8: Totalitarianism and Regime Comparisons

* **Authoritarianism**: political power monopolized; civil society partially tolerated; demands compliance

* **Totalitarianism**: state controls all aspects of life; demands conversion (your mind, not just behavior)
    * Hannah Arendt (*Origins of Totalitarianism*, 1951); Carl Friedrich

* **Hitler**
    * **For**: media, education, youth, economy controlled; SS-Gestapo; racial laws in private life
    * **Against**: churches retained influence (Concordat; Confessing Church); private enterprise; genuine consent 1933–39
    * **[Kershaw]**: came closest but polycracy prevented complete control

* **Castro**
    * **For**: one-party state; media nationalized; CDRs; state economy; UMAP camps
    * **Against**: genuine popular support; Church survived; far lower death toll; sought compliance
    * **[Thomas]**: "authoritarian but not totalitarian"

* **Mao**
    * **For**: controlled production, family, thought, culture; death tolls exceeded Hitler and Castro combined; Little Red Book
    * **Against**: size and rural geography; uneven enforcement; Mao sidelined after Great Leap
    * **[Dikötter]**: "the worst catastrophe in China's history"
    * Closest to totalitarianism in ambition and human cost

* **Economic comparison**

    | Area | Hitler | Castro | Mao |
    | --- | --- | --- | --- |
    | Core approach | Rearmament-driven state capitalism | Nationalization + Soviet dependency | Five-Year Plans, then Great Leap |
    | Unemployment | 6M (1932) → ~300K (1938) | Structural; partially addressed | Five-Year Plan industrialization |
    | Agriculture | Small farms; *Blut und Boden* | Land reform; plantations ended | 90%+ collectivized by 1956; communes |
    | Living standards | Improved 1933–39 | Health/education gains; shortages | Gains wiped out by Great Leap |
    | Weakness | Needed conquest | Soviet dependency; sugar | 15–55 million dead |

### 2.9: Nature of Regime: Hitler

* **Economy**
    * Aims: end unemployment; rearm; **autarky** (self-sufficiency); prepare for war
    * **Reinhardt Programme (June 1933)**: work-creation
    * Autobahn (September 1933)
    * **DAF** (May 1933); **KdF** (November 1933)
    * **Schacht**: Mefo bills; **New Plan (1934)** for bilateral trade
    * **Reich Labour Service (RAD)**: compulsory for young men (1935)
    * **Four-Year Plan (1936)**: under Hermann Göring; synthetic oil/rubber; Schacht resigned November 1937 over pace of rearmament
    * Unemployment below 500,000 by 1938 (rearmament, conscription, women and Jews removed from statistics)
    * Autarky failed; "guns vs. butter"
    * **[Adam Tooze]** (*The Wages of Destruction*, 2006): economy subordinated to rearmament
    * **[Tim Mason]**: economic strain pushed toward war in 1939
    * **[Richard Overy]**: preparing for war in early 1940s; not ready in 1939

* **Women and the family**
    * **Kinder, Küche, Kirche**: children, kitchen, church
    * **Marriage loans (June 1933)**: wife leaves work; 25% forgiven per child
    * Women's university enrollment capped at 10% (1933); barred as judges (1936)
    * **NS-Frauenschaft**: led by Gertrud Scholtz-Klink
    * **Lebensborn (1935)**: SS homes for "racially pure" births
    * **Mother's Cross (1938)**: bronze 4+, silver 6+, gold 8+ children
    * **Sterilization law (July 1933)**: ~400,000 sterilized
    * Birth rate 14.7 → 20.4 per 1,000 (1933–1939)
    * Contradiction: labor shortages → more women working by 1939
    * **[Claudia Koonz]** (*Mothers in the Fatherland*, 1987): women active supporters
    * **[Gisela Bock]**: women primarily victims

* **Youth and education**
    * **Hitler Youth (HJ)** and **BDM**: led by Baldur von Schirach
    * **Hitler Youth Law (1936)**: official; compulsory March 1939; ~8.7 million members
    * **Civil Service Law (April 1933)**: Jewish/"unreliable" teachers dismissed
    * 97% of teachers in National Socialist Teachers' League by 1937
    * Curriculum: race science, Nazi history, PE
    * Elite schools: **Napolas** (1933), **Adolf Hitler Schools** (1937)
    * **Edelweiss Pirates** and **Swing Youth**: nonconformity
    * **[Detlev Peukert]** (*Inside Nazi Germany*, 1987): limits of control
    * **[Michael Kater]** (*Hitler Youth*, 2004): real appeal; effective tool

* **Religion**
    * **Reich Concordat (July 1933)**: Catholic Church keeps schools if out of politics; later violated
    * **Reich Church**: Reich Bishop Ludwig Müller; "German Christians"
    * **Confessing Church**: Barmen Declaration (May 1934)
        * Martin Niemöller in camps 1937–45; Dietrich Bonhoeffer executed April 1945
    * **Mit brennender Sorge (1937)**: Pope Pius XI condemns violations
    * **Bishop von Galen (1941)**: protested killing of disabled
    * Jehovah's Witnesses persecuted
    * **[Kershaw]**: church opposition defended institutions, not the persecuted
    * **[Richard Steigmann-Gall]** (*The Holy Reich*, 2003): many Nazis saw themselves as Christian (contested)

* **Culture and the arts**
    * **Book burnings (May 10, 1933)**
    * **Reich Chamber of Culture (September 1933)**: membership required to work in arts
    * **Degenerate Art exhibition (July 1937)**: vs. Great German Art Exhibition
    * Brain drain: Einstein, Thomas Mann, Brecht
    * Light entertainment also kept loyalty
    * **[Evans]** (*The Third Reich in Power*, 2005): relied on entertainment over constant propaganda
    * **[Kershaw]**: propaganda worked best reinforcing existing beliefs

* **Minorities**
    * **Volksgemeinschaft**: racially "pure" people's community
    * **Boycott of Jewish shops (April 1, 1933)**
    * **Nuremberg Laws (September 1935)**: Reich Citizenship Law; Law for the Protection of German Blood and Honour
    * **Paragraph 175** strengthened (1935): ~100,000 men arrested
    * **Kristallnacht (November 9–10, 1938)**: ~91 killed; ~7,500 businesses destroyed; ~30,000 to camps
    * **T4 euthanasia program (1939–1941)**: ~70,000 killed officially; 200,000+ by 1945
    * Roma persecuted
    * Escalation: legal exclusion (1933–35) → violence (1938) → genocide (1941–45)
    * ~Half of ~500,000 German Jews emigrated by 1939
    * **[Dawidowicz]**: intentionalist
    * **[Kershaw]**: structuralist; builds on Hans Mommsen's "cumulative radicalization"

### 2.10: Nature of Regime: Castro

* **Economy**
    * Aims: end dependence on U.S. and sugar; redistribute; industrialize
    * **First Agrarian Reform (May 1959)**: 400-hectare cap; **INRA** agency
    * Nationalization of U.S. property (August–October 1960); **U.S. embargo** (October 1960; total February 1962)
    * **Che Guevara** Minister of Industries (1961–1965): industrialization failed
    * **Libreta**: rationing book (March 1962)
    * **Second Agrarian Reform (1963)**: 67-hectare cap
    * **Revolutionary Offensive (1968)**: remaining small businesses nationalized
    * **10-million-ton sugar harvest (1970)**: ~8.5 million; missed; Cuba's "Great Leap" without famine
    * **COMECON (1972)**: Soviet sugar-for-oil subsidies
    * **Special Period (1991)**: crisis after USSR collapse
    * **[Thomas]**: social gains, but inefficiency and Soviet dependence
    * **[Gott]**: U.S. embargo forced dependence on USSR

* **Women and the family**
    * **FMC (August 1960)**: led by Vilma Espín
    * **Círculos infantiles**: free childcare (1961)
    * Schools retraining former domestic servants (1961)
    * Abortion legal and free (1965)
    * **Family Code (1975)**: equal housework and childcare by law
    * Labor participation ~13% (1953) → ~38% (1987)
    * Machismo; "double burden"; few in top leadership
    * **[Lois Smith and Alfred Padula]** (*Sex and Revolution*, 1996): real but limited gains
    * **[Gott]**: among the Revolution's most important legacies

* **Youth and education**
    * **1961 Literacy Campaign ("Year of Education")**: ~100,000 **brigadistas**; illiteracy 23.6% → 3.9%
    * Private schools nationalized (June 1961)
    * Free education at all levels
    * **Pioneers (1961)**; **Union of Young Communists (1962)**
    * Schools in the countryside (late 1960s): study + farm work
    * **[Richard Fagen]** (1969): literacy campaign as political socialization
    * **[Thomas]**: one of clearest successes

* **Religion**
    * Bishops' pastoral letters (1960) criticized communism
    * Church schools nationalized (1961)
    * 130+ priests expelled (September 1961)
    * Believers barred from Party until 1991
    * UMAP camps held religious believers
    * *Fidel and Religion* (1985, Frei Betto): softer line
    * **1992**: constitution secular rather than atheist
    * Christmas restored (1997); Pope John Paul II visit (January 1998)
    * **[John Kirk]** (*Between God and the Party*, 1989): confrontation → coexistence
    * **[Thomas]**: pre-1959 Church weakness made sidelining easier

* **Culture and the arts**
    * **ICAIC** (Cuban Film Institute, March 1959); **Casa de las Américas** (April 1959)
    * **"Words to the Intellectuals" (June 1961)**: "within the Revolution, everything; against the Revolution, nothing"
    * *Lunes de Revolución* closed (1961)
    * **UNEAC** (1961)
    * **Padilla Affair (1971)**: Heberto Padilla forced into self-criticism; Sartre, Vargas Llosa protested
    * **"Grey Five Years" (1971–1976)**: tight censorship
    * Alicia Alonso's National Ballet; Nueva Trova
    * **[Lillian Guerra]** (*Visions of Power in Cuba*, 2012): participation + growing control
    * **[Gott]**: genuine achievements in film, music, dance

* **Minorities**
    * March 1959: racial discrimination condemned; public spaces desegregated
    * Afro-Cubans gained from education, healthcare, land, housing
    * 1962: racism declared eliminated; discussion off-limits
    * UMAP camps for gay men; Castro accepted responsibility (2010)
    * **Mariel boatlift (1980)**: ~125,000 left; labeled **escoria** ("scum")
    * **[Alejandro de la Fuente]** (*A Nation for All*, 2001): reduced inequality, but declaring it solved stopped progress
    * **[Carlos Moore]** (1988): preserved white dominance of power

### 2.11: Nature of Regime: Mao

* **Economy**
    * Aims: industrialize; socialize agriculture; surpass Britain in steel in 15 years
    * Land Reform Law (June 1950); 1–2 million landlords killed
    * First Five-Year Plan (1953–1957): real heavy-industry growth
    * Collectivization from 1955; private farming ended by 1956
    * Great Leap: 1958 harvest claimed 375 million tonnes vs. ~200 actual
    * Famine 15–55 million; most estimates 30–45 million
    * Recovery (1961–1965): Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping
    * **[Dikötter]**: crime against humanity
    * **[Jasper Becker]** (*Hungry Ghosts*, 1996): ideological blindness and broken reporting, not deliberate murder
    * **[Meisner]**: failure doesn't erase real transformation

* **Women and the family**
    * **Marriage Law (May 1950)**: banned arranged marriage, concubinage, child betrothal; divorce/property rights
    * Land in women's names
    * Communal kitchens/nurseries in Great Leap
    * "Women hold up half the sky"
    * **Iron Girls**; Jiang Qing as political figure
    * Enforcement eased after 1953 to avoid peasant resistance
    * **[Kay Ann Johnson]** (1983): Party retreated when rights conflicted with rural stability
    * **[Judith Stacey]** (1983): "patriarchal socialism"

* **Youth and education**
    * Literacy ~20% (1949) → ~65% (1965)
    * Simplified characters (1956); pinyin (1958)
    * **Young Pioneers**; **Communist Youth League**
    * Red Guard rally in Tiananmen (August 18, 1966)
    * Universities closed from 1966; entrance exam restored 1977
    * **"Up to the Mountains and Down to the Countryside" (December 1968)**: ~17 million youth
    * "Lost generation"
    * **[MacFarquhar and Schoenhals]** (*Mao's Last Revolution*, 2006): Red Guards unleashed to destroy rivals
    * **[Meisner]**: pre-1966 education brought real gains

* **Religion**
    * Foreign missionaries expelled (early 1950s)
    * **Three-Self Patriotic Movement** (1954); **Chinese Catholic Patriotic Association** (1957; broke with Vatican)
    * **Tibet**: PLA enters (1950); Seventeen-Point Agreement (1951); Dalai Lama flees to India (March 1959)
    * Four Olds (August 1966): temples, churches, mosques destroyed
    * **[Dikötter]** (*The Cultural Revolution*, 2016): massive destruction of heritage
    * **[Tsering Shakya]** (*The Dragon in the Land of Snows*, 1999): destroyed Tibetan structures

* **Culture and the arts**
    * **Yan'an Talks (May 1942)**: art must serve politics
    * Hundred Flowers → Anti-Rightist (~550,000 rightists)
    * **Hai Rui Dismissed from Office** attacked (November 1965): helped launch Cultural Revolution
    * Jiang Qing's **model operas**: only approved stage works
    * **[MacFarquhar]**: Hundred Flowers was genuine uncertainty
    * **[Jung Chang and Jon Halliday]** (*Mao: The Unknown Story*, 2005): deliberate trap (methodology criticized)

* **Minorities**
    * Class enemies: landlords, counter-revolutionaries, rightists
    * Ethnic classification: 55 official minorities
    * **Autonomous regions**: Inner Mongolia (1947), Xinjiang Uyghur (1955), Tibet (1965)
    * Inner Mongolian People's Party purge (1968–1969): tens of thousands killed
    * Official autonomy vs. real Party control
    * **[Shakya]**: autonomy promises not honored
    * **[Dikötter]**: Cultural Revolution especially destructive for minorities

* **Cross-leader links**
    * **Economy**: Hitler kept private ownership under state direction; Castro/Mao nationalized; all subordinated economics to politics (war, revolution, independence)
    * **Women**: Hitler → home; Castro/Mao → workforce; all defined roles to serve the state
    * **Youth**: Hitler trained for war; Castro to teach; Mao to attack authority
    * **Religion**: Hitler co-opted then pressured; Castro marginalized then reconciled; Mao destroyed
    * **Culture**: Hitler and Mao purged; Castro looser until Padilla (1971)
    * **Minorities**: Hitler by race; Mao by class/ethnicity; Castro proclaimed racial equality but excluded gay men/dissenters

## Unit 3: The Move to Global War

### 3.1: Causes of Japanese Expansion

* **Nationalism and militarism**
    * **Meiji Constitution (1889)**: Emperor held supreme command of army/navy (Article 11)
    * **Right of supreme command**: General Staffs reported to Emperor, not PM/cabinet
    * Services could topple a cabinet by withholding an Army/Navy Minister
    * **Kokutai**: loyalty to Emperor/nation over party politics
    * **Kwantung Army**: guarded leased territory and South Manchuria Railway; killed warlord Zhang Zuolin (June 1928) without Tokyo's approval
    * **"The Dark Valley"**: civilian government weakening as military gained control

* **Resentment of the Western-led order**
    * **1919**: racial equality clause blocked at Paris
    * **Washington Naval Treaty (1922)**: battleship ratio 5:5:3 (US:UK:Japan); wanted 70%, got 60%
    * Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902) lapsed; **Nine-Power Treaty**: "Open Door" in China
    * **1924 U.S. Immigration Act**: barred Japanese immigration
    * **London Naval Treaty (1930)**: limits extended to cruisers; ratified over Navy General Staff's objection
        * Called an "infringement of the right of supreme command"
        * PM Hamaguchi Osachi shot November 14, 1930; died August 1931

* **Great Depression**
    * Raw silk (largest export, mainly to U.S.) collapsed
    * **January 1930**: returned to gold standard; worsened slump
    * Rice prices fell; northern famine (1931)
    * Rural army recruits; officers blamed politicians and **zaibatsu** (big business)
    * **Manchuria**: coal (Fushun), iron (Anshan), soybeans, farmland, railway
        * Matsuoka Yōsuke: Manchuria and Mongolia Japan's "lifeline" (January 1931)

* **Political instability in China**
    * **1928**: Chiang Kai-shek's GMD unified China on paper (Northern Expedition 1926–28)
    * Real power with warlords; Manchuria ruled by **Zhang Xueliang** ("Young Marshal"), joined GMD December 1928
    * **Central Plains War (1930)**: Zhang's best troops moved south of the Great Wall
    * Chiang's priority: destroy CCP first (Jiangxi Soviet campaigns from December 1930)
    * Chinese nationalists threatened Japanese interests; Nakamura and Wanpaoshan incidents (summer 1931)
    * Divided China → invasion looked low-risk



<br>



### 3.2: The Mukden Incident and the Lytton Commission

* **Mukden Incident (September 18, 1931)**
    * Kwantung Army staged explosion on South Manchuria Railway near Mukden (**Liutiao Lake incident**)
    * Blamed on Chinese soldiers; pretext for invasion
    * Not authorized by Tokyo; **fait accompli**
    * September–November 1931: rapid occupation of Manchuria
    * **[Ian Nish]**: Kwantung Army acted independently; civilian-military tension decisive

* **Occupation to Manchukuo**
    * **December 1931**: League Council sends commission of inquiry
    * **January 1932 Stimson Doctrine**: U.S. won't recognize territorial changes by force (U.S. policy, not League)
    * **February–March 1932**: puppet state **Manchukuo**; Puyi (last Qing emperor) as figurehead
    * **September 1932**: Japan recognizes Manchukuo before Lytton Report published

* **Lytton Commission**
    * Chaired by Victor Bulwer-Lytton (Britain); Gen. Frank McCoy (USA, observer-member); Gen. Henri Claudel (France); Count Luigi Aldrovandi Marescotti (Italy); Heinrich Schnee (Germany)
    * Investigated Japan, China, Manchuria in 1932
    * Signed at Peiping September 4, 1932; published October 1932
    * Deliberately balanced

* **Findings**
    * September 18–19 operations not legitimate self-defense
    * Manchukuo not a genuine independence movement; existed because of Japanese troops
    * Japan had real "special interests"; Chinese disorder/boycotts genuine grievances
    * **Recommendations**: Japanese forces back to railway zone; Manchuria autonomous under Chinese sovereignty; new China-Japan treaties
    * **Did not**: demand withdrawal by force, call for sanctions (Article 16), harshly brand Japan aggressor

* **Ten conditions (Chapter IX) in four groups**
    * **Balance** (serve both sides): (1) compatible with China's and Japan's interests; (4) recognition of Japan's interests in Manchuria; (9) economic rapprochement
    * **Law** (respect the wider order): (2) consideration for USSR's interests; (3) conformity with League Covenant, Kellogg-Briand Pact, Nine-Power Treaty
    * **Autonomy** (new set-up for Manchuria): (7) Manchurian autonomy within Chinese sovereignty; (8) local gendarmerie for internal order; security against external aggression
    * **Lasting peace** (prevent a repeat): (5) new China-Japan treaties; (6) provision for settling future disputes; (10) international cooperation in Chinese reconstruction

* **Responses**
    * **League (February 1933)**: Assembly adopts report 42–1 (Japan against); Manchukuo illegitimate; non-recognition; no sanctions; no force
    * **Japan**: delegation walks out; withdraws from League March 27, 1933; pushes into Jehol (early 1933); Puyi crowned Emperor (1934)
    * **Britain and France**: Depression recovery first; protecting Hong Kong, Indochina, China trade; no sanctions
    * **U.S.**: not a League member; moral condemnation only
    * **China**: appealed repeatedly; sympathy, no military support

* **Structure vs. choice**
    * **Structural (couldn't)**: no standing army; unanimity needed; U.S. outside League so sanctions would leak
    * **Political (wouldn't)**: Britain/France prioritized recovery; feared for Asian colonies/trade; unwilling to enforce
    * **[F.S. Northedge]**: League structurally incapable of enforcement
    * **[Sally Marks]**: Britain and France had leverage and chose not to use it
    * **[Akira Iriye]**: expansion rooted in a regional imperial order
    * **Precedent**: Japan defied the League and kept Manchuria → Abyssinia (1935), Rhineland (1936), China (1937)
